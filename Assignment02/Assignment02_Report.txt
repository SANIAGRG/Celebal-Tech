1. DATASET OVERVIEW

For this assignment, I worked with the Sample Superstore dataset — a popular
retail dataset that captures four years of US order transactions (2014–2017),
with 9,994 order line items spread across 21 columns. It covers everything
from customer and product details to sales figures, discounts, and profit
values, making it a great fit for SQL-based business analysis.

  Total Revenue    : ~$2.30 million      Unique Orders   : ~5,009
  Total Profit     : ~$286,000           Unique Customers: 793
  Overall Margin   : ~12.5%              Missing Values  : 0
  Geography        : 49 US states, 4 regions

The key columns I worked with throughout the analysis were: Order Date,
Ship Date, Region, Category, Sub-Category, Sales, Quantity, Discount,
Profit, Segment, and Ship Mode.


2. APPROACH

The dataset was loaded into an in-memory SQLite database using Python —
specifically pandas for reading the CSV and sqlite3 for the SQL engine.
Before loading, I cleaned up the column names (replacing spaces and hyphens
with underscores, and lowercasing everything) so they would work cleanly
in SQL queries. I also wrote a small helper function to run any query and
return the result as a readable DataFrame, which kept the notebook tidy
throughout.

The analysis was structured into 7 steps:

  1. Load the CSV into SQLite
  2. Explore the schema, sample rows, and summary statistics
  3. Filter with WHERE across region, category, date range, and sales value
  4. Aggregate with GROUP BY to compare sales and profit by region,
     category, segment, and ship mode
  5. Rank results with ORDER BY and LIMIT — top products, top states,
     and worst-performing sub-categories
  6. Answer 8 specific business questions: monthly trends, year-over-year
     category growth, top customers, discount impact on profit, loss-making
     orders, shipping performance, duplicate detection, and a region-by-
     category cross-tabulation
  7. Validate data quality — checking for NULLs, negative sales, invalid
     discounts, date logic issues, and row count accuracy

SQL features used throughout: SELECT, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, ORDER BY,
SUM, AVG, COUNT, CASE WHEN, strftime(), julianday(), ROW_NUMBER() OVER(),
subqueries, and pandas pivoting for cross-tabulation. Five charts were also
generated using matplotlib to visually support the key findings.


3. CHALLENGES AND HOW I SOLVED THEM

Every project has its friction points, and this one was no different. Here
is what I ran into and how I worked through each issue:

  1. CSV Encoding Error
     The file uses latin1 encoding, not UTF-8, which caused a
     UnicodeDecodeError on the first load attempt. Adding encoding='latin1'
     to pd.read_csv() fixed it immediately.

  2. SQL-Unfriendly Column Names
     Column names like "Order Date" and "Sub-Category" break SQL syntax.
     I cleaned them all — replaced spaces and hyphens with underscores
     and lowercased everything — making them safe and easy to query.

  3. Date Format Mismatch
     Dates in the CSV were in MM/DD/YYYY format. SQLite treats them as text,
     so date-range filters would silently return wrong results without a
     format fix. I used pd.to_datetime() to convert them to YYYY-MM-DD
     (ISO 8601) before loading, which SQLite handles correctly.

  4. Notebook Cell ID Warning
     nbconvert flagged a MissingIDFieldWarning on every cell because the
     notebook was missing the 'id' field that nbformat 5.1+ requires. I
     wrote a short script using Python's json and uuid modules to add a
     unique UUID to every cell and update the nbformat version. Warning gone.

  5. In-Memory Database Resets on Kernel Restart
     SQLite in-memory databases don't persist across sessions. This is an
     accepted trade-off for the simplicity and portability it offers — I
     structured the notebook so the first few cells always recreate the
     database, keeping everything reproducible.


4. KEY FINDINGS

  1. Revenue grew 51% from 2014 to 2017, but the profit margin stayed flat
     at around 12% throughout — growth without improving efficiency.

  2. The West region leads in both sales and profit. The Central region is
     the outlier — decent sales volume, but the weakest profit margin of
     all four regions.

  3. Technology generates the highest revenue and profit overall. Office
     Supplies has the best margin efficiency. Furniture is the problem area.

  4. Tables and Bookcases are loss-making sub-categories, dragged down by
     average discounts of 30% or more, which wipe out any margin entirely.

  5. Discounts above 20% consistently push orders into net loss territory —
     this was the single most important finding in the analysis.

  6. Q4 (November and December) is the peak season every year without
     exception. January and February are consistently the slowest months.

  7. The top 10 customers account for a disproportionately large share of
     revenue. Sean Miller alone has spent over $25,000 — more than three
     times the average customer.

  8. Data quality is excellent — zero NULLs and no invalid values across
     all 9,994 rows, which made it easy to focus on analysis rather than
     cleaning.

6. RECOMMENDATIONS

  1. Cap discounts at 20% — beyond this point, orders lose money on average.
     This single policy change could meaningfully improve overall margins.

  2. Review Tables and Bookcases pricing — both sub-categories are currently
     loss-making and need either price increases or stricter discount limits.

  3. Continue investing in Technology, especially in the West — it offers
     the best combination of revenue volume and profit margin.

  4. Investigate why the Central region's profit lags so far behind its
     sales volume — discounting practices or product mix may be the cause.

  5. Plan Q4 inventory and marketing well in advance — the seasonal spike
     is predictable every year and should be prepared for, not reacted to.

  6. Review high-discount accounts like Sean Miller to assess whether
     their long-term purchase value actually justifies the reduced margin.
